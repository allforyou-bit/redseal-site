The 100-question bank, area by area

The free PDF is a 50-question slice of a 100-question pool, organized by the topic areas gas apprentices are examined on. Here is how those 100 break down, what sits inside each area, and the mistakes that come up again and again.

  • Appliances & Equipment — 22 questions The biggest area because it is the widest: furnaces, boilers, water heaters, fireplaces and dryers, plus the components inside them — thermocouples, combination gas valves, hot surface ignitors, inducer motors, rollout limit switches, aquastats, dip tubes. Much of it is control-sequence knowledge: what proves flame, what breaks the circuit, what order a boiler is commissioned in. The usual miss is naming a component's purpose but not the condition that operates it — plenty of apprentices can point to a rollout switch and not say what trips it.
  • Gas Supply Systems — 18 questions Everything upstream of the appliance: service and appliance regulators, two-stage propane regulation, lockup pressure, excess flow valves, meters, odourant, and the gas properties that drive the design — specific gravity, heating value per cubic foot, propane vapour pressure against temperature. Propane and natural gas behave differently at nearly every one of those points, and mixing the two up is the largest source of lost marks here. Anything involving propane in cold weather is a reliable stumbling block.
  • Piping Systems — 16 questions Material selection, approved thread sealant, drip legs, support spacing, sleeving through foundation walls, underground corrosion protection, and CSST bonding. Bonding is the one that catches people: apprentices know it is required but describe it as ordinary grounding continuity rather than protection against lightning-induced arcing, which is the hazard it actually addresses. Sizing is the other trap — pressure drop rising with flow is where a shop rule of thumb stops working.
  • Venting Systems — 16 questions The four appliance venting categories and what each demands, B-vent against sealed combustion against condensing-appliance plastic vent, connector pitch, common venting of two appliances, clearances from a termination to windows, doors and a roof surface, and spillage testing. The category system is where marks go: Category I and Category IV get mixed up because both are common in the field, and vent material follows directly from the category.
  • Combustion Theory — 14 questions Stoichiometric combustion, primary against secondary air, what excess air does to efficiency, flame appearance as a diagnostic, flue-gas CO2 and stack temperature readings, the conditions that generate carbon monoxide, and flame rectification. This area rewards understanding over memory: if you can explain why excess air raises stack temperature and lowers efficiency, most of the analyser questions answer themselves. Memorising a target CO2 figure without the relationship behind it fails as soon as the numbers change.
  • Safety & Code — 14 questions Leak response and when a building gets evacuated, what CSA B149.1 governs, carbon monoxide symptoms and detector placement, permits, purging a new system before lighting appliances, and the scope difference between a Class A and a Class B licence. Many of these are sequence questions rather than fact questions — "what is the FIRST action" has one correct order, and a sensible-sounding step in the wrong position is still marked wrong.

Working through the 50 in one sitting

Sit the whole 50 with a clock running and the code book shut. That second part matters more in gas than in most trades: on the job you reach for B149.1 without thinking, but a written exam tests what you carry in your head.

Mark every question you guessed, score it, then sort the misses into two piles. One pile is numbers you did not know — clearances, pressures, support spacing, CO2 targets. That pile is flashcard work and it clears quickly. The other pile is mechanisms you did not understand: why CSST is bonded, why venting category dictates material, why excess air costs efficiency. That pile is slower, and it is the one most apprentices skip.

Watch for a propane-versus-natural-gas pattern running through your wrong answers. If they concentrate on LP questions across Gas Supply and Piping, that is one gap, not six. The same goes for venting: if Category III and IV questions cost you marks, relearn the category table once rather than the individual questions.

How gasfitter certification is regulated in Canada

Gas work is regulated province by province, and the picture is not uniform. Gasfitter — Class A is a Red Seal designated trade in Alberta, British Columbia, Manitoba, New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia, the Northwest Territories, Nunavut, Prince Edward Island, Quebec and Yukon. It is not on the Red Seal designation list for Ontario or Saskatchewan, and Red Seal states that exams built on the newer occupational standard for this trade are still under development.

Ontario certifies gas work through its provincial fuels safety regime rather than through Red Seal designation. Certificates are issued under Ontario Regulation 215/01 in categories including G.3, G.2 and G.1, training is delivered by accredited providers using an approved curriculum, and a G.3 holder works under the general supervision of a G.1, G.2 or DA certificate holder.

So check what your own jurisdiction issues before planning around a certificate name. The six topic areas in this bank are the working knowledge base behind gas certification wherever you are, because CSA B149.1 is the common code reference across the country. But this is independent study material: it carries no credit, satisfies no requirement, and working through it does not lead to a Red Seal endorsement or a provincial gas certificate. For the same reason this page prints no question count, time limit or passing mark for any gasfitter exam — those are set by the issuing jurisdiction and they vary.

Questions gas apprentices ask about this PDF

  • Does it cover propane as well as natural gas? Yes, both run right through the bank. Propane turns up in supply (two-stage regulation, vapour pressure, tank behaviour in cold weather), in piping (why copper is restricted for LP), and in the combustion figures, because the properties differ at almost every point.
  • Which code edition do the questions follow? They work from CSA B149.1 concepts that hold across editions, but specific clearances and requirements can change with the edition your province has adopted. Check the current adopted edition and its amendments before treating any figure as final.
  • Is this useful for Ontario G.2 or G.3 study? The underlying knowledge overlaps heavily, since B149.1 is the common reference. But Ontario's certification follows an approved curriculum with its own defined scope per category, so treat this as supplementary practice, not a substitute for accredited training.
