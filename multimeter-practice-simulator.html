    Practise with an example digital multimeter on a 12 V lamp circuit: set the dial and jacks, measure volts, amps and ohms, and see what each mistake does.

    This free simulator puts an example digital multimeter on a 12 V circuit: battery, fuse holder, switch, connector and one lamp. In Measure, set the dial and jacks and place the leads; in Drills, put a wrong setup right and pick what went wrong. The Red Seal standards for the 421A, 309A and 442A trades list multimeters among their tools and test equipment.

    Dial and jacks

    A digital multimeter combines a voltmeter, an ammeter and an ohmmeter, and its dial picks the function. The black lead goes in COM for every function; for volts the red lead goes in the V jack, which on some meters also serves resistance and continuity. For current the red lead goes to the jack the meter assigns to current, which may differ from the volts jack; the manual of the meter in hand decides.

    Set the function and range and plug in the leads before touching the circuit, keep the settings while testing, and take the red lead off the circuit first.

    Voltage: across a part

    Voltage is measured across a part, one lead on each side, and a minus sign on DC volts means the leads are reversed. Most digital multimeters have a voltage input resistance far higher than the parts they read, so placed across a circuit they barely load it.

    With the lamp on, this example meter reads 12.52 V across the battery posts and 0.02 V across the fitted fuse, the closed switch or a battery cable. Across an opening, such as the empty fuse holder, an open switch or a parted connector, this example meter reads 12.60 V with the lamp off.

    Current: in series

    Current is measured in series: open the circuit and insert the meter so all of the current flows through it. With the power off, open the circuit, put the red lead in a current jack rated for the expected current, set DC amps and bridge the opening, then power up and read. Afterwards turn the power off, remove the meter and restore the circuit.

    Across the empty fuse holder this example meter reads 3.86 A and the lamp lights; with the leads swapped it reads −3.86 A. An ammeter connected across a part, in parallel with it, is a short circuit and can damage the meter and the circuit. Across the closed switch this example meter reads 0.08 A and the lamp stays bright, because most of the current still takes the switch.

    The fused current input

    With a lead left in a current jack, touching the leads across a voltage shorts the source through the meter, and high current flows. Many meters fuse or otherwise protect their current inputs against such a fault, but not every meter does; its manual says which inputs are fused. Never measure voltage with a lead in a current jack, and move the red lead back to the volts jack after current work.

    Replace a meter fuse with the type and rating the meter maker specifies, and check the fuses before measuring current. A meter whose current input is not fused should be kept off high-energy circuits.

    In this example meter the fused A jack is so low in resistance that leads across the battery open the meter fuse whatever the dial says; across the lit lamp, the circuit fuse opens instead.

    Resistance and continuity

    On resistance the meter drives its own small current from an internal battery, so measure with the power off and the part removed or isolated. Measuring resistance on a powered circuit can damage the meter or the circuit; this example meter shows a message instead.

    Continuity is a go or no-go test: a beep means a low-resistance path, which may run through a load, and no tone a broken path or too much resistance. The resistance that sets off the beep varies from model to model.

    Touching the probe tips together checks the leads: in this example that reads 0.2 Ω, and the lead resistance is part of every resistance reading. A cold lamp filament has far lower resistance than a lit one, so continuity can beep through a lamp: in this example, with the battery off, lamp positive terminal to chassis reads 0.6 Ω and beeps.

    Reading the display

    Some meters need the range set by hand and others pick it automatically; OL means the value is beyond what the meter can show. On resistance and continuity, OL means more resistance than the meter can measure, as with open leads or an open circuit. On the 6 V range of this example meter the battery shows OL, and the 60 V range or Auto reads it.

    When setting a voltage range by hand, start at a high range. Resolution depends on the range: across the closed switch this example meter shows 0.016 V on its 6 V range and 0.02 V on its 60 V range.

    Proving the meter

    In lockout on building power, which this page does not cover, qualified workers treat a circuit as live until tested dead, checking the tester on a known source before and after. Testing the known source again confirms that the meter was still working when it read the circuit.

    In the Red Seal standards for 309A and 442A, lockout includes testing for zero potential or a zero energy state with testing equipment, which includes voltmeters.

    Measurement categories

    Categories rate meters for building power circuits, which this page does not measure; see the scope note under About this simulator.

    A measurement category, defined for test equipment in the IEC 61010 series, classifies a meter’s measuring circuits by the type of building supply they connect to. CAT IV covers the source of the installation, such as the service entrance; CAT III the distribution part of a building, such as panels; CAT II equipment that plugs into the building wiring. Sources place ordinary wall receptacles differently, so this page does not give them a category.

    Choose the category first and the voltage second: a higher voltage rating does not make up for a lower category. The meter, leads and probes must be rated for the category and voltage present, and provincial regulators name the standards they accept, such as CAN/CSA-C22.2 No. 61010-1. The example 12 V board is not on building power, so no building location above describes it; a meter later used on building power must be rated for that location.

    Clamp meters and true RMS

    A clamp-on ammeter reads current from the magnetic field around a conductor, without opening the circuit. A true-rms meter reads distorted AC accurately up to its specified crest factor; an average-responding meter is accurate on a pure sine wave and can read much lower on distorted waveforms.

    Battery safety

    Where the chassis is grounded to the battery negative, as in this example circuit, disconnect the negative cable to the chassis first and reconnect it last. While the negative cable is connected, a metal object touching the positive side and the chassis at the same time closes a circuit and sparks, and with enough hydrogen from the battery, a spark can cause an explosion. In this simulator, disconnecting the battery opens the negative cable alone.

    Parts of the board and the meter

    Battery and cables

    The 12 V battery supplies the board: its positive cable runs to the fuse holder and its negative cable to chassis. The Battery connected control opens or closes the negative cable.

    Fuse and holder

    The fuse protects the lamp feed and can be pulled from its holder to be checked on its own. A fuse opens when the current through it is too high for its rating.

    Switch

    The switch opens and closes the lamp feed.

    Connector

    The connector joins the feed wire to the lamp and can be parted, which opens the circuit without cutting a wire.

    Lamp

    The lamp is the load, and how brightly it lights shows how much current flows through it.

    Feed and ground wires

    The feed wire runs from the switch to the connector, and the ground wire from the lamp to chassis.

    Chassis ground

    Chassis ground is the return path to the battery negative cable.

    Display

    The display shows the reading, OL, or a message that explains why there is no reading.

    Dial

    The dial of this example meter selects OFF, DC volts, AC volts, resistance, continuity or DC amps.

    COM jack

    COM takes the black lead for every function.

    VΩ jack

    On this example meter the VΩ jack takes the red lead for volts, resistance and continuity.

    A jack

    The A jack takes the red lead for current, and on this example meter its path runs through the meter fuse.

    Test leads

    The test leads connect the meter to the circuit; check them by touching the probe tips together on resistance.

    Meter fuse

    The meter fuse sits inside this example meter, in the path of the A jack, and opens when that path carries too much current.

    Dial on AC volts for a DC battery: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    A battery is a DC source, so the AC setting has nothing to read here.

    How to tell it apart

    With the dial on DC volts the same leads read the battery.

    Test leads reversed: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    The meter shows the red lead against the black lead, so swapping them flips the sign.

    How to tell it apart

    The size of the reading stays the same and the sign changes.

    Red lead left in the A jack for a voltage reading: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    Through the A jack the meter is a very low resistance, so across a voltage it is a short.

    How to tell it apart

    In the VΩ jack the same leads read the voltage and no fuse opens.

    Battery cannot hold its voltage under load: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    A weak battery loses more of its voltage inside itself as current flows.

    How to tell it apart

    In this example circuit a weak battery reads 11.47 V with the lamp on and 12.60 V with the switch off, since its voltage falls as the current it delivers rises.

    Meter on DC amps across a closed switch: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    Most of the current takes the closed switch, so little of it goes through the meter.

    How to tell it apart

    With the switch open the meter carries the whole lamp current.

    Red lead in the VΩ jack for a current reading: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    Current goes through the A jack path of this example meter, not through the VΩ jack.

    How to tell it apart

    With the red lead in the A jack the same setup reads the current.

    Meter fuse already open: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    With the meter fuse open the A jack path carries no current.

    How to tell it apart

    A new meter fuse brings the current reading back.

    Meter on DC volts connected in series: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    This example meter on DC volts lets very little current through, so in series it reads nearly the supply and the lamp goes out.

    How to tell it apart

    On DC amps through the A jack the lamp lights and the meter reads its current.

    Lamp burned out: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    An open lamp stops the current in the whole loop.

    How to tell it apart

    A setup mistake leaves the lamp lit once it is put right, while a failed lamp stays dark.

    Circuit fuse open: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    An opened fuse is an open path, so the meter shows OL across it.

    How to tell it apart

    In this example circuit an intact fuse reads 0.2 Ω, close to the lead check.

    Broken test lead: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    A broken lead is an open path in the meter leads.

    How to tell it apart

    With new leads the tips read the lead check and the battery reads again.

    Resistance measured with the circuit powered: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    On resistance the meter drives its own current, which a powered circuit upsets, and measuring this way can damage the meter or the circuit.

    How to tell it apart

    With the battery disconnected the same leads read a resistance.

    Continuity used where a resistance value was needed: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    Continuity is a go or no-go check: the beep says the path is complete, and the resistance setting is the one for reading how much resistance a part has.

    How to tell it apart

    On resistance the same path reads a value with no beep.

    Continuity path through the lamp to chassis: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    A cold lamp reads low, so continuity beeps through it.

    How to tell it apart

    A real short to chassis still beeps with the connector parted.

    Feed wire shorted to chassis: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    A short gives a path to chassis that does not go through the lamp.

    How to tell it apart

    Without a short the parted connector leaves OL.

    Manual range set below the voltage: why, and how to tell it apart

    Why

    On this example meter a range shows readings up to its top, and above it the display shows OL.

    How to tell it apart

    On the 60 V range or Auto the same leads read the battery.

    About this simulator

    The example meter and circuit are generic, not any product; the board has no AC source, and the meter no clamp or milliamp input. Values to compare a real meter or circuit against come from its manual and service information. In this model a lit lamp is a fixed resistor with a lower cold value, while a real filament changes resistance as it heats. A fuse here opens as soon as its steady current is above its rating, while a real fuse opens faster the larger the overcurrent. On a real circuit, make electrical measurements only with specific instructions from a qualified person, and disconnect the battery before replacing components. 120 V AC power circuits are not covered here; where lockout is not practicable, live measurement on them is work for authorized, qualified workers following written procedures, with protective equipment chosen for the hazard, for example using CSA Z462.

    Frequently asked questions

    What does OL mean on a multimeter?

    OL means the value is beyond what the meter can show; on resistance, more resistance than the meter can measure, as with an open circuit.

    Why does a multimeter fuse blow?

    A current-input fuse opens when more current flows through that input than its rating, as when leads left in a current jack touch across a voltage. Replace the fuse with the type and rating the meter maker specifies.

    How is current measured with a multimeter?

    On a 12 V circuit like this example board, not building wiring, turn the power off, open the circuit and put the meter in series so all the current flows through it. Use a current jack rated for the expected current and the amps setting, and bridge the opening with the leads before the power comes back on.

    How is a fuse checked with a multimeter?

    With the power off, pull the fuse and read across it on resistance or continuity. An opened fuse reads OL and an intact one reads low; in this example circuit that is 0.2 Ω, the same as the lead check.

    What does CAT III mean on a multimeter?

    CAT III marks a meter built to withstand the transients of circuits in the distribution part of a building, such as panels and feeders; the category and the voltage beside it together set that withstand. Choose the category first and the voltage rating second, and use leads and probes rated for at least the same category and voltage.

    Why is resistance measured with the power off?

    On resistance the meter sends its own small current through the part, so any voltage from the circuit upsets the reading and can damage the meter or the circuit. Continuity is a power-off test too, since it is a resistance test, and nearby circuits may still be live, so keep the probes off energized parts.
