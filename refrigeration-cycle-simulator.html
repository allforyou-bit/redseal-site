Practise on example refrigeration systems: change the charge and entering air temperatures, read superheat and subcooling, and find the fault.

This free simulator runs an example refrigeration system with a fixed orifice or a thermostatic expansion valve (TXV), two gauges and two line thermometers. In Operate, change the charge and the temperature of the air entering each coil, and read superheat and subcooling; in Find the fault, take readings and choose the cause. It is practice for the refrigeration cycle and troubleshooting questions of this site’s 313A refrigeration and air conditioning mechanic practice exam.

How the cycle works

The cycle has four main parts on two pressure levels: the compressor and the metering device change the pressure, and the condenser and the evaporator move heat. Refrigerant takes in heat in the evaporator at low pressure and gives it off in the condenser at high pressure. In the basic cycle, the condenser gives off the heat taken in by the evaporator plus the work of the compressor.

Pressure and temperature

For a refrigerant with no glide, where liquid and vapour are both present, the pressure sets the temperature: each saturation pressure goes with one saturation temperature. So the suction gauge gives the evaporating temperature and the head gauge the condensing temperature. A vapour or a liquid away from saturation can sit at many temperatures at one pressure, which is why superheat and subcooling need a line temperature too.

For a blend with high temperature glide, superheat uses the dew point and subcooling the bubble point; this example refrigerant has no glide.

Superheat and subcooling

Superheat is the suction line temperature at the evaporator outlet minus the evaporating temperature read from the suction gauge. Subcooling is the condensing temperature read from the head gauge minus the liquid line temperature at the condenser outlet.

Zero superheat: the evaporator outlet is wet and liquid can reach the compressor. Zero subcooling: the liquid line can carry flash gas.

Superheat and subcooling on a real system are judged against the equipment maker’s figure. In the simulator each superheat and subcooling readout shows its subtraction, with the set-up value beside it.

Fixed orifice and TXV

On the fixed-orifice example, less charge raises superheat and lowers subcooling, and more charge does the reverse. On the TXV example, superheat moves little as the charge changes, while subcooling follows the charge.

More load on the evaporator raises superheat on the fixed-orifice example; on the TXV example the valve opens further and superheat moves little. Warmer air entering the condenser raises the condensing temperature and the head pressure on both examples.

Try each row in Operate on one metering device, then on the other; warmer air entering the evaporator adds load.

Finding the fault

Find the fault uses the TXV example: each scene puts one fault into it, and every scene starts from the same symptom. The system is running. Take readings and compare them with set-up.

Gauges, Suction line and Liquid line come before a cause can be chosen; Amps, Evaporator air and Condenser air add readings for comparison. A wrong pick gets the reading that rules it out; a cause these readings cannot rule out is marked not ruled out, and the scene goes on.

Not every cause on this list has a scene in this version. A compressor that pumps poorly is not among the faults in this version.

Parts of the example system

Compressor

What it does: Draws vapour from the suction line and pumps it to the condenser at high pressure.

When it goes wrong: A compressor that pumps poorly is not among the faults in this version.

Condenser

What it does: Gives off heat to the air flowing over it, so the vapour condenses to liquid; the last part of the coil cools that liquid below its condensing temperature.

When it goes wrong: Low airflow across it, as from a dirty coil, is one of the causes in Find the fault.

Condenser fan

What it does: Moves air across the condenser; the Operate row for the condenser changes how warm the entering air is, not how much air the fan moves.

When it goes wrong: A fan that moves too little air gives low airflow across the condenser.

Fixed orifice

What it does: A fixed restriction at the evaporator inlet that drops the refrigerant to the low-side pressure.

When it goes wrong: Find the fault uses the TXV example, so this version has no fixed-orifice fault.

Thermostatic expansion valve (TXV)

What it does: Meters refrigerant into the evaporator, opening or closing with the superheat at the evaporator outlet, where its sensing bulb is clamped.

When it goes wrong: A valve whose sensing bulb has lost its charge underfeeds the evaporator; that is one of the causes in Find the fault.

Sensing bulb

What it does: Clamped to the suction line at the evaporator outlet, it senses the temperature there and works the valve through its capillary.

When it goes wrong: A bulb that has lost its charge leaves the valve underfeeding.

Evaporator

What it does: Takes heat from the air flowing over it as the refrigerant boils; at set-up the refrigerant is all vapour by the outlet, and the warming past the boiling point is the superheat.

When it goes wrong: Low airflow across it is one of the causes in Find the fault.

Evaporator fan

What it does: Moves air across the evaporator; the Operate row for the evaporator changes how warm the entering air is, not how much air the fan moves.

When it goes wrong: A fan that moves too little air gives low airflow across the evaporator.

Discharge line

What it does: Carries high-pressure vapour from the compressor to the condenser.

When it goes wrong: No discharge line fault is modelled, and this version shows no discharge line temperature.

Liquid line

What it does: Carries liquid from the condenser to the metering device; its temperature at the condenser outlet gives the subcooling.

When it goes wrong: A blocked filter drier restricts the liquid line; it is one of the causes in Find the fault.

Suction line

What it does: Carries vapour from the evaporator to the compressor; its temperature at the evaporator outlet gives the superheat.

When it goes wrong: No suction line fault is modelled in this version.

Suction gauge

What it does: Reads the low-side pressure, called suction pressure, shown here as the evaporating temperature.

When it goes wrong: This version has no gauge faults.

Head gauge

What it does: Reads the high-side pressure, called head pressure, shown here as the condensing temperature.

When it goes wrong: This version has no gauge faults.

About this model

Example refrigerant and example systems, made up for this simulator. The example systems have no receiver or accumulator, the model leaves out moisture on the coils, and every reading is for steady running.

Compressor amps in this model are worked out from the compressor power and shown against a set-up mark, not in amperes. Gauge needles, amps and cooling are shown against a set-up mark, not as pressures or amperes. A change inside a small band either side of the set-up value reads about the same.

Diagnosis and charging on a real system follow the equipment maker’s service information.

Safety and handling

Work on a real system follows the equipment maker’s service information and the safety rules where the work is done. Who may handle halocarbon refrigerants is set by the regulations where the work is done. Releasing halocarbon refrigerants is regulated, and the rules where the work is done apply.

Frequently asked questions

What is superheat?

Superheat is the suction line temperature at the evaporator outlet minus the evaporating temperature read from the suction gauge. Superheat and subcooling on a real system are judged against the equipment maker’s figure.

What is subcooling?

Subcooling is the condensing temperature read from the head gauge minus the liquid line temperature at the condenser outlet. Zero subcooling: the liquid line can carry flash gas.

Why does a charge fault show in subcooling on this TXV example?

This example has no receiver; its valve opens or closes to hold superheat, so the charge shows mainly in subcooling. Makers’ tables agree that subcooling falls with an undercharge and rises with an overcharge; they differ on superheat for an overcharge.

How is an overcharge told from low airflow across the condenser?

Both raise head pressure. Subcooling rises with an overcharge; with low condenser airflow, makers’ tables give it about the same or lower.

Why does a valve that has lost its bulb charge lower both pressures in this example?

It lets too little refrigerant through, so suction pressure falls with the starved evaporator; in this example head pressure falls too. Makers’ tables give both lower for a lost bulb charge but differ on head pressure for a restricted liquid line.

Can this simulator’s readings tell air in the condenser from an overcharge or low airflow across the condenser?

Not in this simulator. Air in the condenser also raises head pressure. Nothing in this simulator separates them.

Can this simulator’s readings tell a blocked filter drier from a valve that has lost its bulb charge?

Not in this simulator. An instrument maker’s table gives both the same readings; another maker’s table gives head pressure higher for a blocked drier. Nothing in this simulator separates the two.

Which trade is this simulator for?

It is built for the 313A refrigeration and air conditioning mechanic exam; the other trades have their own practice pages on this site.
