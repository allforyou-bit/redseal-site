Question Bank Updates

A dated record of what was corrected in the practice question banks, in the PDFs made from them, and in the exam facts stated on this site.

Updated October 2026

How corrections are made

In September 2026 all eleven question banks were audited. Each finding was put to a reviewer whose job was to defend the question, and most of the findings that survived were checked again by a second, independent reviewer.

  • Wrong or disputable answer: the question is removed. It is not edited until it passes. Removed questions leave the free quiz, the free pages and the PDFs.
  • Wrong explanation: the explanation is corrected. The corrected line is written from a source the corrector opened and is checked by an independent verifier before it goes live. The question, its options and its answer stay the same.
  • New questions go live only after a break attempt and a source check. They replace a question that tested the same fact as another, or bring a bank back to its official exam length. A reviewer tries to break each one and its source is checked independently; candidates that fail are dropped, not revised.
  • Findings that are not defects are dismissed. Those questions are left as they were.

Later corrections are added to this page with their date. To report an error, use the Contact page.

421A Heavy Duty Equipment Technician

Bank size: 421 before the audit, 406 after.

  • 2026-10-19 3 questions changed: one wrong option was reworded in each, and the questions, answers and explanations are unchanged.   • 2026-10-16 1 question replaced and 6 explanations or key concepts corrected. Torque converter: the old question had coupling begin at about 90% of impeller speed; the new question in its place asks what happens as turbine speed comes close to pump speed (the stator freewheels and the converter works as a fluid coupling), as Allison’s operator manuals describe it, with no fixed percentage. Supplemental coolant additive: a single range of 1.5–3.0 units per gallon, tested at every service, was removed because the range depends on the additive and the coolant; the explanation now gives Cummins’ statements that under-concentration leads to liner pitting and over-concentration to additive drop-out, plugged radiator tubes and overheating. Charging circuit voltage drop: the test is now described as Delco Remy prints it, with a carbon pile loading the circuit to the alternator’s rated output and both sides added together, in place of an engine-speed test with limits that had no source. Wording that a topic appears regularly on safety exams and that a step is often skipped was removed.

313A Refrigeration & Air Conditioning Mechanic

Bank size: 116 before the audit, 115 after.

  • 2026-10-28 1 explanation and its key concept corrected; the question, options and answer are unchanged. Capillary tube when the evaporator load rises: the explanation opened by saying the tube passes whatever the pressure difference across it will push through a fixed bore, and the key concept said flow follows the pressure difference across a fixed bore; neither had a source. Both now say only what the Danfoss article they cite states: the restriction is fixed, so the device cannot open further when the load rises.
  • 2026-10-21 3 questions changed. Capillary tube when the evaporator load rises: the keyed choice is the same, and the answer now says the tube cannot adjust its restriction because its bore and length are fixed; it no longer repeats the question’s word “respond” and is no longer the shortest option. The explanation said a capillary tube does not meter, although the question calls it a metering device; it now says the tube does not modulate. Statements that flow depends on the tube’s length and that capacity falls short were removed, and so was the list of consequences (critical charge, no receiver, accumulator, sensitivity to charge error). The explanation now quotes Danfoss (ASHRAE Journal, February 2020): a fixed orifice device will not open more when the load increases, which raises the superheat, and when the load decreases the risk of liquid reaching the compressor rises; a TXV modulates to hold superheat constant. The key concept now says “no moving parts” of capillary tubes, where it said it of all fixed orifices. Compressor restart timer: the question now describes a single-phase condensing unit with a reciprocating compressor whose timer keeps the compressor off for at least five minutes. The answer is the same choice, now worded as Rheem’s installation instructions put it: a start at unequalized pressures that trips the overload or blows a fuse. The explanation follows the makers: Rheem (single-phase PSC units off for a minimum of 5 minutes so the pressures equalize, which the time delay control in the low-voltage circuit enforces; start components with non-bleed expansion valve coils on its reciprocating-compressor models) and Danfoss (a low-starting-torque device with capillary tube control and pressure equalizing, a high-starting-torque relay and start capacitor for expansion valve control or capillary control without equalizing). Scroll compressors are read maker by maker: Copeland says its ZP scrolls start unloaded with no minimum off time, apart from a short lock-out of single-phase scrolls after brief power interruptions, and Rheem keeps the 5-minute off time on its 14AJM scroll units. Statements that a fixed orifice equalizes in a couple of minutes, that TXV systems carry a hard start kit and that the timer caps starts per hour were removed. R-410A replacement: the question was rewritten. It rested on a claim about common use, and its answer named two refrigerants (R-32 or R-454B) and was the only option to repeat the question’s word “lower-GWP”.
